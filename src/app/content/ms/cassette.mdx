During the early 1700s, girls and women were sent from France were sent to the French colony of Louisiana to become the wives of settlers. They arrived in several groups throughout the 18th century, and under a variety of circumstances – mostly orphaned and or falsely imprisoned-  with their ships often  stopping at Ship Island, a barrier Island off the Gulf Coast, before arriving in the colonies.  The island isn’t visible from land, but you can get a close look from one of the ferries leaving the Jones Park Ferry Terminal. The first group of girls arrived in 1704 and were called the Pelican Girls, after the ship that carried them across the ocean.

In 1719, 96 women arrived on the La Mutine, or "the Mutanious Woman". The vast majority of these women were taken from the La Force sector of the Pitié-Salpêtrière, a Parisian hopsiptal that also functioned as an asylum, hopsice and prison.

They were “among the poorest Parisians” and had “been arrested only very recently, always in Paris, often by the same law enforcement officers”. Their journey to the colony was especially difficult. They were chained and dressed only in rags. Once in the colonies, they were left on Ship Island for an unspecified amount of time. After leaving Ship Island and arriving in Biloxi, they dispersed across the colonies into Mobile and New Orleans. The majority of these women were married to colonists, but this was not without doubts about their respectability, which has reappeared in the media in the past centuries.

In 1721, 88 women arrived in Biloxi, MS, on the La Baleine. These girls were brought under the guidance of Sister Gertrude and two other conductresses.

These girls were supposed to have been carefully selected among the orphans of the Hôpital Général. The women who arrived on the La Baleine are often the women who are labeled as the Cassette Girls, identified by the small trunk of apparel provided by the Company of the Indies. In a letter written by André Pénicaut, he states that “each one has a dower for the supposed marriage, two pairs of coats, two shirts and undershirts, six-head dresses, and other furnishings with which they were amply provided so that they might marry with all possible dispatch”.
The first women to arrive in the French colony of Louisiana are often seen as mythical figures, with their stories made to be scandalous, mysterious, or vampiric. In New Orleans, the Cassette Girls or Casket Girls are rumored to be vampires who live on the third floor of the Ursuline Convent. Dramatic and romantic fictional tales about Cassette Girls can be found in media, such as the novel <i>The Story of the Chevalier des Grieux and Manon Lescaut</i>, as well as the operetta and movie <i>Naughty Marietta</i>. The Cassette Girls have also been dramatically depicted in paintings. This is evident in <i>Departure for the Island</i> by Pierre Dupin and Antoine Watteau, and <i>Manon Lescaut</i> by Maurice Leloir. The difficult and inspiring stories of these women and girls have been warped throughout time, leaving harsh realities behind for tales of fiction.
