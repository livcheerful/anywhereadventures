When Andrew Carnegie immigrated to America in 1848 at the age of 12, he had completed very little formal education. In his autobiography, he had this to say about his academic skills at that point: "I had left school forever, except one winter's night-schooling in America, and later a French night-teacher for a time, and, strange to say, an elocutionist from whom I learned how to declaim. I could read, write, and cipher, and had begun the study of algebra and of Latin."

Andrew started his first job as a bobbin boy in the Anchor Cotton Mill near Slabtown, a slum area of Allegheny City (now Pittsburgh's Northside). Slabtown was also called Barefoot Square by locals to describe the extreme poverty of families whose children roamed the streets that "had no municipal water system until 1848, no gas lines until 1853, and no sewer system. Wild hogs roamed the streets."

Soon after turning 13, Andrew was offered a job as a telegraph messenger boy at a telegraph office located in Pittsburgh at the corner of Fourth and Wood. Taking this job elevated him from the 13-hour day, 6 days a week, to what he described as his first real start in life.

"From the dark cellar running a steam-engine at two dollars a week, begrimed with coal dirt, without a trace of the elevating influences of life, I was lifted into paradise, yes, heaven, as it seemed to me, with newspapers, pens, pencils, and sunshine about me. There was scarcely a minute in which I could not learn something or find out how much there was to learn and how little I knew. I felt that my foot was upon the ladder and that I was bound to climb."

Colonel James Anderson, a wealthy iron merchant living in Manchester, near Slabtown, mentored Andrew and his friends by creating a Mechanics' and Apprentices' Library for "working boys" using his 400-volume personal library.

Andrew would go every Saturday afternoon, return one book, then select another. Carnegie expressed his debt to Anderson by funding a monument in front of the
Allegheny Carnegie Library. Anderson so inspired the young Carnegie that he later donated funds for a series of Carnegie Free Libraries that would number 2,509, including some belonging to public and university library systems, across the country. The monument is inscribed with the following tribute to his friend and mentor.

"To Colonel James Anderson, Founder of Free Libraries in Western Pennsylvania. He opened his library to the working boys and on Saturday afternoons acted as librarian, thus dedicating not only his books, but himself, to the noble work. This monument is erected in grateful remembrance by Andrew Carnegie, one of the ‘working boys’ to whom were thus opened the precious treasures of knowledge
and imagination through which youth may ascend. "
