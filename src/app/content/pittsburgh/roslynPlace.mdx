Pittsburgh is notable for many things, such as the Ferris Wheel, the first commercial radio broadcast, and the birthplace of the emoticon (:-0). 

It is also home to the last remaining wooden road, which was a paving method that was more cost effective in the 1850s.

Pavement technology evolved in tandem with methods of transportation -- as the load that the road had to bear increased, so did the durability and effectiveness of the road. Unpaved dirt roads were troublesome for horses and other pack animals, especially when rain would seep in and transform the dirt into mud. If cities had the resources to do so, they would pave their thoroughfares to create easily traversable channels.

The materials used for pavement changed over time. Stones were originally used to smooth the way - from antiquity to the 1700s. Engineers and towns started to experiment with the design of the roads by adding layers of different kinds of stones to distribute the weight better or sloping the road from the center to improve drainage.

Stone as a building material had several downsides -- cobblestone roads were bumpy and relatively expensive -- whereas wood was readily available and could be cut into a smooth surface. Nicolson pavement, one of the techniques for building roads out of wood, cut the wood into blocks that would be inlaid into the road.

However, there were many, many downsides to this technique. The treatment for the wood to improve its longevity would cause it to emit an unpleasant smell. It also had all the shortcomings of trees: it was flammable, and if a fire were to break out, it would act as a line of fuel from building to building. It would also float in the case of a flood, as it did in Paris in 1910. And even if it wasn’t enough rain to cause a flood, the slightly damp surface would be extremely slippery and caused both humans and horses to fall.

Nowadays, roads are born out of large engineering efforts: a combination of sand and rock held together by asphalt or cement. You can find examples of cobblestone and Belgian block in many places around the world -- surviving due to their longevity. But Roslyn Place is one of the only places where you will be able to find the fallible Nicolson pavement.
