A specialty vehicle designed for emerency patient transport and care hadn't been created yet: instead, people were put in the back of police vans where it was difficult to provide medical care en route.

However, even this service wasn’t always available, especially to residents in the primarily Black neigbhorhoods of Pittsburgh. For folks living in the Hill District, the police would be slow to respond to their calls, to the point where it was difficult to expect them to come at all.

In response to this, and receiving both local and national funding, in 1967 a new program was created by Phil Hallen and Dr. Peter Safar to create a new specialty ambulance program to specifically hire from and service the Hill District and other neighborhoods that found the police's care unreliable.

The program was an overwhelming success. Freedom House developed an 8 month training program to cover medical aid, CPR, and other emergency care techniques. Trainees were taught how to intubate and defillibrate patients in the ambulance, a level of en-route care that had never been practiced before. To join this inaugural program, a cohort of 25 Black folks were hired from the Hill District and trained under this program to become EMTs. With only two ambulances deployed in the first year, the Freedom House team saved 200 lives and responded to 5,800 calls (averaging to 15 calls a day).

The practices developed by the Freedom House Ambulance Service were immediately adopted by cities around the US, like Miami, Los Angeles, and Jacksonville. It continued to mold the future of emergency care across the country, and the ambulance they designed became the de facto model for the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.
