A specialty vehicle designed for emergency patient transport and care hadn't been created yet: instead, people were put in the back of police vans where it was difficult to provide medical care en route.

However, even this service wasn’t always available, especially to residents in the primarily Black neighborhoods of Pittsburgh. For folks living in the Hill District, the police would be slow to respond to their calls, to the point where it was difficult to expect them to come at all.

In response to this, Drs. Phil Hallen and Peter Safar, created a new specialty ambulance program using both local and national funding. The program, founded in 1967, specifically hired drivers from and serviced the Hill District and other neighborhoods that found the police's services unreliable.

The program, called Freedom House, was an overwhelming success. As part of the program, trainees underwent 8-months of training including medical aid, CPR, and other emergency care techniques. Trainees were taught how to intubate patients and how to use a defibrillator while in the ambulance, a level of en-route care that had never been practiced before. The program's inaugural cohort was a group of 25 Black residents hired from the Hill District. With only two ambulances deployed in the first year, the Freedom House team saved 200 lives and responded to 5,800 calls (averaging to 15 calls a day).

The practices developed by the Freedom House Ambulance Service were immediately adopted by cities around the US, like Miami, Los Angeles, and Jacksonville. The program continued to mold the future of emergency care across the country, and the ambulance they designed became the de facto model for the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.
